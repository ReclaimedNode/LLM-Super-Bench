THE PATIENT MACHINE
An original essay written for this repository and dedicated to the public domain (CC0 1.0).
Replace this file, or drop your own text beside it and point race.config.json at that instead.

I.

A computer is a patient machine. It will do the same small thing several billion times in a
second and never once wonder why. That patience is the whole trick. Nothing a computer does is
clever in the way a person is clever; the cleverness lives in the arrangement, in the order we
put the small things in, and in the sheer willingness of the machine to keep going long after
any person would have stopped to ask what the point was.

The first people to build these machines understood this better than we sometimes do. They were
not trying to make a mind. They were trying to make an arithmetic that did not get tired. A
person adding a column of figures makes roughly one mistake in every few hundred lines, and the
mistakes are not random: they cluster around the end of the afternoon, around interruptions,
around the moment the adder starts thinking about dinner. A machine that adds has none of those
failure modes. It has others, and they are strange ones, but they are not those.

So the bargain was struck early. We would give up intuition and get back consistency. We would
give up the ability to notice that a number looked wrong, and get back the ability to handle
more numbers than any person could look at. Every argument about computers since has been an
argument about that bargain and where its edges are.

II.

Consider how a signal travels. You press a key. A small spring closes a small circuit. Somewhere
a controller notices the change, and because it was already looking, it notices within a few
thousandths of a second. It writes a number into a place set aside for such numbers. A program,
which has been waiting, finds the number, decides it means the letter you meant, and hands it on.
By the time the letter appears on the screen, the signal has passed through perhaps a dozen
translations, each one faithful, none of them aware of the others.

This is the part that people find hardest to believe: there is no one in charge. There is no
small clerk inside the machine who understands that you are writing a letter to your sister. There
are only layers, each of which knows how to speak to the layer above it and the layer below it,
and none of which knows the purpose of the whole. The purpose lives outside the machine. It lives
in you.

Networks work the same way, only the distances are longer and the faith required is greater. A
message leaves your house in pieces. Each piece carries an address and very little else. No piece
knows the route it will take. Routers along the way make a fresh decision for each one, based on
what they knew a moment ago about which direction was good, and their knowledge is always slightly
out of date. Pieces arrive out of order. Some do not arrive at all. At the far end, a program
sorts them back into sequence, asks politely for the missing ones, and presents the result as if
it had travelled in one calm piece, which it never did.

The remarkable thing is not that this occasionally fails. The remarkable thing is that it works at
all, continuously, for billions of people, on equipment made by companies that do not speak to one
another, running software written decades apart. It works because the agreements are simple enough
to be implemented correctly by people who never meet, and because every layer is willing to be
ignorant of every other.

III.

Speed is the subject people most enjoy arguing about, and the subject about which they are most
often confidently wrong. Here is the difficulty. A machine's speed is not one number. It is at
least three, and they trade against one another.

The first is latency: how long until anything happens at all. You press the key; how long until
the letter appears. This is the number people feel. A system can do an enormous amount of work per
second and still feel slow, because the first thing it does, it does late. Latency is dominated by
the things that must happen in sequence, one after another, with no way to overlap them: a
round trip across a continent, a disk head moving, a model reading an entire question before it
may begin its answer.

The second is throughput: how much gets done once things are moving. Throughput is where parallelism
lives. If a task can be cut into pieces that do not depend on one another, throughput can be bought
almost with money — add more workers, get more done. Latency cannot be bought this way. Nine women
cannot produce a baby in one month, as the old line has it, and a thousand processors cannot shorten
a chain of a thousand steps that each depend on the last.

The third is consistency: how much the first two vary. A system that answers in one second every
time is, for most human purposes, better than one that usually answers in a tenth of a second and
occasionally takes thirty. People plan around the worst case they have experienced, not the average
they were promised. This is why the honest way to describe a system's speed is to give a
distribution, or at the very least a median and a bad case, rather than a single triumphant number
from the best run of the afternoon.

Almost every misleading performance claim comes from quoting one of these three and implying the
others. A benchmark that measures throughput on a warm cache and reports it as responsiveness is
not lying about any particular number. It is lying about which number matters.

IV.

Memory is the quiet constraint behind all of it. A processor can only work on what is close to
hand, and "close" is a physical fact, not a metaphor: electricity takes time to cross a board, and
at these speeds the time is significant. So machines are built as a series of shrinking rooms. A
few values sit in registers, immediately available. A few thousand sit in a cache that can be read
in a handful of cycles. A few billion sit in main memory, reachable in a few hundred. Beyond that
lies storage, which is another world entirely, slower by a factor that used to be a hundred
thousand and is now merely a thousand.

Most of the art of making software fast is really the art of arranging for the next thing you need
to already be in a small room. Programs that respect this are often dull to read: loops that walk
straight through memory in order, data laid out in flat blocks, few pointers to chase. Programs
that ignore it can be beautiful on the page and hopeless in practice, wandering from room to room,
spending all their time walking and none of it working.

This is also why the size of a problem changes its character. A thing that fits in cache and a
thing that does not are not the same thing made bigger; they are different things. The methods that
win at one scale lose at another. Anyone who has watched a tidy algorithm fall apart when the input
grew by a factor of ten has met this fact in person.

V.

And then there is the part that is not technical at all.

Every machine is built by people, and it carries their decisions in its bones long after they have
gone. The reason a particular file format has a strange field in it, the reason a protocol reserves
a byte nobody uses, the reason a system will not accept a name longer than a certain length: these
are fossils. Each one was a reasonable judgement on a Tuesday, made by somebody who expected to
revisit it and never did. To read an old system closely is to read a record of other people's
Tuesdays.

This is not a complaint. It is the condition of building anything that lasts. The alternative to
carrying old decisions is breaking everything that depends on them, and everything depends on them.
So we build forward, adding rooms to houses whose foundations we did not pour, and we try to leave
our own decisions in a state where the next person can understand what we were thinking.

The best engineers I have known are distinguished not by knowing more facts but by holding a better
model of where they are ignorant. They know which part of the system they understand, which part
they have merely used successfully, and which part they are simply trusting. When something breaks,
they look first at the boundary between those regions, because that is nearly always where it
broke.

VI.

What should a person take from all this?

Perhaps only that the machine is not magic, and that its lack of magic is what makes it useful. It
does small things faithfully. It does them in an order somebody chose. Its speed has three faces
that must be named separately or not at all. Its memory is a set of shrinking rooms. Its history is
a pile of other people's Tuesdays, and its behaviour at the edges is where its truth lives.

None of this requires a talent for mathematics to understand. It requires only the willingness to
ask, at every layer, what exactly is happening, and to keep asking after the first satisfying
answer. The machine will wait. It is patient. That was always the point.
